The Structural Operating Deficit

USU has run a structural operating deficit every year we have data for. It has widened from $218M (FY2009) to $307M (FY2021). The university survives because non-operating revenues (state appropriations + endowment distributions) fill the gap. The endowment alone covers roughly 8% of that gap. This is the fragility the standard investment policy does not account for.

Investment Income Volatility

Annual investment income has swung from -$1.4M (FY2009) to +$74.2M. This range is 3x the annual endowment payout. The spending policy assumes this volatility is manageable. The risk model tests that assumption.

Net Position Growth

Total institutional net position has grown at 7.6% CAGR over 15 years. But most of this is illiquid capital assets (buildings, land). The endowment is the only meaningful liquid buffer.

The FY2009 lesson: During the 2008 financial crisis, USU's investment income went negative (-$1,443,025). That was a single-sector financial crisis lasting roughly 18 months. The scenarios modeled in the Risk Model tab involve compounding, multi-sector stress over years, not months. FY2009 is the closest historical reference point USU has for correlated portfolio failure, and the conditions described in the risk scenarios are broader and longer-lasting.
The 8.5% Threshold

Under Policy 5000.4.2, USU's endowment must generate enough return to cover a 4% spending rate, a 1.5% administration and advancement fee, and roughly 3% inflation to maintain real purchasing power. That totals 8.5% annually, every year, in perpetuity. When returns fall below that line, the endowment is being consumed. Re: Endowment Strategy and Structural Risk at Utah State University

Dear Scott,

I graduated from Utah State in 2004 with a degree in economics. I have spent the years since working in finance and business at an international level. I am writing because I care about this institution and the communities it serves, and because I believe the question this letter raises deserves to be put in front of the people responsible for answering it.

Over the past two years, working with colleagues in the United States and internationally, I have been developing a systemic risk modeling framework with the specific aim of making cascading institutional risks visible, testable, and adjustable. The results, applied to USU's own financial record, are striking enough that I feel a responsibility to share them directly.

This is not a solicitation. I am not selling anything. I have tried to keep this analysis grounded in USU's own audited financial record. I believe the data speaks clearly enough that it warrants a conversation.

Analysis — March 2026

I. Introduction

The world's leading institutional risk organizations, including Swiss Re, the IMF, the World Bank, the World Economic Forum, and the V-Dem Institute, have spent the past several years documenting a convergence of structural stresses across insurance markets, sovereign debt, food systems, democratic governance, and energy. Taken individually, each trend is concerning. Taken together, and modeled with attention to how they interact under stress, the implications for institutions like USU are severe. This memo presents that analysis, grounded in USU's own 15-year financial record.

The analysis draws on that body of institutional research and on a scenario modeling framework built and tested with financial professionals and academics working in systemic risk. I have also built a tool that makes every underlying assumption transparent and adjustable, so that anyone can interrogate the calibration directly rather than take my word for it.

I anticipate that a memo raising concerns about investment strategy will generate predictable objections. I have tried to address the most important ones directly in Section VII, using USU's own audited record as the evidentiary base.

Top Level Conclusion

The analysis finds that the structural conditions now building across energy, insurance, food systems, and sovereign debt markets are, in several respects, more severe than those that produced the FY2009 moment, and the assumptions embedded in USU's current endowment strategy do not appear to account for correlated multi-system disruption of that kind.

USU's endowment strategy assumes its three primary revenue sources, investment returns, state appropriations, and private giving, behave independently under stress. They do not. In FY2009, all three compressed simultaneously. The structural conditions now building are more severe than those that produced that moment. Under current calibration, the conventional endowment strategy produces a median Year 10 outcome of $27M against a $1,390M target. The sections that follow explain why, and what a different path looks like.

II. Where USU Stands Financially

The endowment is well-managed by conventional measures:

Endowment value: $614.9M (NACUBO FY2024)
Investment return FY2024: 10.0%, beating the 9.1% benchmark
Annual payout: approximately $24.6M (4% spending rate on a 12-quarter moving average)
Annual fundraising: $68.8M (FY2025), with $26.8M in net new gifts to endowment

Under Policy 5000.4.2, sustaining real purchasing power while meeting distribution commitments requires approximately 8.5% nominal annual return. Over 10 years, that compounds to a target endowment value of $1,390M.

USU's audited financial reports, reviewed across 15 years, reveal something the headline numbers do not: a structural financial position that is more fragile than it appears, and that becomes acutely exposed under the conditions this letter describes.

III. The Structural Picture USU's Records Show

Across every year from FY2009 to FY2021, USU has run an operating deficit of between $185M and $307M annually. That deficit has widened every year without exception, growing 41% over the period. USU covers it through two non-operating sources: state appropriations and investment returns. Those two sources account for roughly 90% of what keeps the institution solvent on an annual basis.

This is not a criticism of management. It is the structural reality of how large public research universities operate. But it has a direct implication for endowment strategy that the Investment Policy Statement does not appear to address: the two pillars that close USU's operating gap are not independent. They compress under the same conditions.

The 2008 crisis is the clearest demonstration of the mechanism. When markets deteriorated, investment income turned negative. The economic shock that caused the market downturn simultaneously reduced private giving and forced state budget cuts. None of these effects were independent. They were the same event expressing itself across three different revenue streams at once.

In FY2020, net position grew $84.8M despite COVID. This may seem to refute the concern. It does not. Investment income fell 38% ($42.5M to $26.3M). State appropriations were cut. Operating losses widened to -$274.5M. The institution stayed solvent that year because of federal relief funding, a one-time intervention that will likely not repeat in the next disruption cycle.

IV. The Assumptions Inside the Investment Policy

For USU's endowment to deliver 8.5% nominal returns consistently over the next 10 years, all of the following would need to hold simultaneously:

Equity markets sustain positive real returns without a prolonged structural disruption

The insurance sector stays functional across western US geographies, despite accelerating climate-driven withdrawal already underway

Sovereign debt markets remain orderly, despite G7 debt-to-GDP ratios at historical extremes

Agricultural systems do not suffer structural shocks to soil productivity, water availability, or supply chain concentration

The broader institutional environment remains stable enough for capital to function

The difficulty is not that any one of these is implausible. The difficulty is that they are all required at the same time, and they are not independent of each other. When one begins to fail, it tends to accelerate failure in the others. That is precisely the dynamic standard portfolio construction is not built to model.

USU's own history demonstrates this. In FY2009, the breakdown was not slow or sequential. It was simultaneous and self-reinforcing. Markets fell, gifts fell, state revenues fell. The system did not absorb the shock and recover gracefully. It ran out of shock absorbers at the same moment across all three revenue streams.

The conditions building now are structurally similar, and in several respects more severe. Energy cost instability, water system contraction in the western US, and institutional trust erosion are not hypothetical future risks. They are observable 2026 conditions.

V. What the Model Shows

We built a Monte Carlo state-transition model that runs 5,000 simulations across five world-states: Stable Growth, Stagnation, Crisis, Systemic Breakdown, and Recovery. It incorporates 12 disruption vectors drawn from peer-reviewed research, each with its own probability, duration, and correlation structure. Calibrated to observable 2026 conditions, the central results are:

Probability of Severe or worse world-state by Year 10: 80.4%
Probability of Crisis conditions: 75.7%
Substrate Health Index at Year 10: 34 out of 100, reflecting cumulative degradation of the physical, institutional, and social foundations that all asset values rest on

VI. The Land-Grant Problem

USU is not a generic endowment. Its statutory mission is the advancement of agriculture, natural resources, and the applied sciences that support physical productivity. That mission is not at arm's length from the risks this model identifies. It sits at the center of them.

The two highest-probability disruption vectors in the USU scenario are food system shock, at 65% cumulative probability over 10 years, and climate tipping signal, at 60%. Those are not abstract portfolio risks. They are direct threats to what USU exists to do. If soil productivity deteriorates structurally, if western water systems contract, if agricultural supply chains fail in concentrated ways, USU's research mission becomes more urgent precisely when the endowment's capacity to fund it is most compromised.

The conditions most likely to strain the endowment are the same conditions that most directly threaten what USU is for. A fiduciary committee should know whether those two things are on a collision course.

VII. Questions Worth Asking Before We Meet

I expect several responses to this letter. The most predictable ones are addressed directly below, using USU's own record.

"The FY2024 return was 10%, beating benchmark."

One strong year does not alter the structural picture. The endowment earned negative investment income in FY2009. It earned $15M in FY2012, an implied return of roughly 5.4% on invested assets. The range across the years we have data for runs from -$1.4M to +$74M. A strategy that requires 8.5% every year for 10 years cannot tolerate that kind of volatility. The question is not whether the endowment can have a good year. It is whether the strategy is built for a bad decade.

"We survived COVID."

USU survived COVID because of federal relief funding, not because the portfolio absorbed the shock. Investment income fell 38% in FY2020. Operating losses widened. The federal backstop papered over the correlated revenue collapse. It is not a replicable mechanism. The next shock will not come with a similar fiscal response.

"Our investment advisors run stress tests."

Standard stress tests model historical shocks in isolation: a 2008-style market event, a 1970s inflation episode. They do not model simultaneous multi-system disruption in which conventional correlation assumptions break down. That is a different analytical problem. I would welcome the chance to compare the methodology your advisors use against the framework built here, specifically on the question of correlated failure across investment returns, state revenues, and private giving.

"Alternative investments are speculative."

The approach described below is not speculative. It is investment in physical and institutional infrastructure that holds value because it produces things people need regardless of financial market conditions: food, water, energy, local supply chain capacity. The speculative position is the assumption that conventional assets will perform as they have historically across conditions that are structurally different from the historical period.

VIII. What a Different Path Looks Like

I am not suggesting USU abandon its investment strategy. I am suggesting there is a category of asset allocation worth understanding, one built around resilience in an unstable environment rather than return maximization in a stable one.

In a world where the conditions described above are materializing, the assets most likely to hold value are tied to things people actually need: local food systems, water infrastructure, community energy, regional supply chains, and institutional capacity to coordinate around shared problems. These are not exotic investments. They are the physical and social substrate that conventional finance treats as background, but which actually determines whether any other asset class continues to function.

In practice, a reoriented strategy for USU would include:

Direct investment in regional food infrastructure (processing, storage, distribution) that strengthens the agricultural economy USU's research mission depends on

Community-scale energy and water infrastructure that reduces exposure to grid fragility and western water contraction

Patient capital in local enterprises that build economic resilience in Cache Valley and the communities USU serves

Institutional capacity investments whose value is highest precisely when conventional financial markets are under stress

The model shows this orientation producing a median Year 10 outcome of $684M under the USU Continuity preset. Not the $1,390M target, but dramatically better than $27M, and achieved by investing in the same systems USU's land-grant mission exists to strengthen. The endowment and the mission would be pulling in the same direction.

I recognize this raises implementation questions that require exactly the expertise your committee has. I am not here with a finished product. I am here with a direction, a logic, and deep systems analysis.

IX. What We Are Asking For

Three things, in sequence.

First: examine the model. The link above takes you directly to the USU Continuity preset. Every assumption is visible and adjustable. If your read of current conditions differs from ours, the model will show you what that means for the numbers. Push back on the calibration. The differences are worth discussing.

Second: ask your investment advisors to run a stress test that models simultaneous multi-system disruption: not a single-variable historical shock, but a scenario in which investment returns, state appropriations, and private giving compress at the same time. If they have done that work, we would welcome the chance to compare notes. If they have not, it is worth asking why, given that USU's FY2009 record shows exactly that dynamic.

Third: meet. I will bring the full analysis and the underlying research. I am not here to tell the committee what to do. I am here because I think the question deserves to be asked in front of the people responsible for answering it.

This university has served this community for well over a century. The land-grant mission is not abstract. It is the reason people in Cache Valley have access to research and extension knowledge that makes a difference in a hard year. We want that to continue. The question this analysis raises is whether the endowment strategy currently in place is built to make that possible across the conditions most likely to test it.

I believe it deserves a serious look. I hope you agree.

Model Methodology

This is a simplified scenario-based Monte Carlo model. It is designed for accessibility and transparency, not for actuarial precision.

How it works: For each scenario, the model generates N simulation runs (default 1,000). Each run draws annual returns from a normal distribution defined by the scenario's mean return and volatility. The endowment value is updated each year: previous value * (1 + return) - spending draw. Spending draw is calculated as a percentage of the prior year's value. Results are aggregated to produce median paths and percentile bands.

Scenario definitions: Each scenario represents a different set of assumptions about the macro environment. Baseline uses historical USU return parameters. Stress scenarios apply return drags and increased volatility based on published institutional risk assessments (see sources above). The cascade chain for each scenario shows the causal mechanism, not just the return assumption.

What this model does NOT do: It does not model individual disruption vectors (climate, insurance, fiscal) separately. It does not include compounding damage logic or substrate health tracking. It does not model recovery dynamics. For all of these features, see the full Systemic Risk Model.

Why the ranges are wider: A simplified model that aggregates disruption into a single return distribution will naturally produce wider confidence intervals than a model that tracks individual mechanisms. This is a feature, not a limitation, for this audience. The message is: even with generous bands, the risk is material.
